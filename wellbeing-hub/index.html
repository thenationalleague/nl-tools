Looking after your mental health

Everyone has mental health. It can change over time and may be affected by pressure, injury, relationships, work, education and events away from football.

You may find this page helpful if…

  • You feel different from your usual self
  • You are finding it harder to enjoy football or daily life
  • Your sleep, appetite, energy or concentration have changed
  • You want practical ways to protect your wellbeing

In football

Why this matters

Good mental health supports concentration, confidence, decision-making, relationships, recovery and enjoyment of football. Difficulties can affect anyone, regardless of their role or level in the game.

Signs to look out for

  • Persistent worry, sadness or irritability
  • Changes in sleep, appetite or energy
  • Withdrawing from teammates, colleagues, friends or family
  • Loss of motivation or enjoyment
  • Using alcohol, drugs, gambling or other behaviours to cope

Things you can do today

  • Talk to someone you trust
  • Keep regular routines for sleep, food, hydration and movement
  • Break problems into smaller, manageable steps
  • Stay connected with people outside football
  • Seek professional help if difficulties persist or affect everyday life

When should I seek help?

  • Symptoms have lasted for around two weeks or more
  • They are affecting football, work, education, relationships or daily life
  • They are getting worse or your usual coping strategies are not helping
  • You are worried about yourself or somebody else

Support inside football

  • Club Welfare Officer
  • Trusted manager, coach or colleague
  • Club medical or player-care team

Support outside football

  • Your GP
  • NHS Talking Therapies
  • Samaritans — 116 123

Stress and anxiety

Stress is the body's response to pressure. Anxiety can involve continuing worry, fear or physical symptoms even after the immediate pressure has passed.

You may find this page helpful if…

  • You are struggling to switch off
  • Football or life feels overwhelming
  • You are constantly worrying
  • You have physical symptoms such as tension or a racing heart

In football

Why this matters

Selection, contracts, performance, injury, travel, social media and balancing football with work, education or family life can all create pressure.

Signs to look out for

  • Racing thoughts or constant worry
  • Tension, headaches, nausea or a racing heart
  • Poor sleep
  • Irritability or avoiding situations
  • Difficulty concentrating or making decisions

Things you can do today

  • Slow your breathing and focus on the present
  • Write down what is within your control
  • Plan recovery time after matches and travel
  • Reduce caffeine and avoid using alcohol to cope
  • Speak to someone early rather than waiting for a crisis

When should I seek help?

  • Symptoms have lasted for around two weeks or more
  • They are affecting football, work, education, relationships or daily life
  • They are getting worse or your usual coping strategies are not helping
  • You are worried about yourself or somebody else

Support inside football

  • Club Welfare Officer
  • Manager or coach
  • Club medical team

Support outside football

  • Your GP
  • NHS Talking Therapies
  • Mind

Depression and low mood

Low mood is common after disappointment or stress. Depression is more persistent and can affect thoughts, sleep, appetite, confidence, relationships and everyday functioning.

You may find this page helpful if…

  • You have felt low for a while
  • You have lost interest in things you normally enjoy
  • You feel hopeless, guilty or worthless
  • Your mood is affecting football, work or relationships

In football

Why this matters

Release, injury, non-selection, criticism and uncertainty can affect identity and confidence. Depression is an illness, not a lack of toughness or commitment.

Signs to look out for

  • Low mood most days
  • Loss of interest or enjoyment
  • Hopelessness, guilt or feeling worthless
  • Low energy and poor concentration
  • Thoughts of death, suicide or self-harm

Things you can do today

  • Tell someone what is happening
  • Maintain basic routines even when motivation is low
  • Avoid isolating yourself
  • Book an appointment with your GP
  • Use urgent help immediately if you may harm yourself

When should I seek help?

  • Symptoms have lasted for around two weeks or more
  • They are affecting football, work, education, relationships or daily life
  • They are getting worse or your usual coping strategies are not helping
  • You are worried about yourself or somebody else

Support inside football

  • Club Welfare Officer
  • Trusted club contact
  • Club medical or player-care staff

Support outside football

Suicidal thoughts and crisis support

Suicidal thoughts can affect anyone and should always be taken seriously. Help is available and nobody should manage this alone.

You may find this page helpful if…

  • You are thinking about suicide or self-harm
  • You feel trapped, hopeless or unable to cope
  • You are worried that someone else may be unsafe
  • You need urgent support now

In football

Why this matters

Football pressures may add to personal difficulties, but suicide is rarely caused by one issue. A caring and direct conversation can help someone access support.

Signs to look out for

  • Talking about wanting to die or being a burden
  • Feeling trapped, hopeless or unable to cope
  • Giving away possessions or saying goodbye
  • Sudden withdrawal or a major change in behaviour
  • Making plans or searching for methods

Things you can do today

  • Call 999 if there is immediate danger
  • Go to A&E or contact NHS 111 for urgent help
  • Stay with the person where it is safe to do so
  • Listen calmly and take them seriously
  • Do not promise secrecy when somebody may be at risk

When should I seek help?

  • Now. Do not wait to see whether it passes.
  • Do not wait until you are certain it is serious enough.
  • If you are not safe, or not sure you are safe, use the emergency numbers below.
  • If you are worried about somebody else, speak to them today.

Support inside football

  • Club Welfare Officer
  • Club safeguarding lead
  • Trusted senior club contact

Support outside football

  • Emergency services — 999
  • Nearest A&E
  • NHS 111

Sleep and recovery

Sleep supports physical recovery, concentration, mood, immunity and decision-making.

You may find this page helpful if…

  • You are struggling to fall asleep or stay asleep
  • You feel exhausted during the day
  • Your mood or concentration is affected
  • Travel, evening fixtures or worry are disrupting recovery

In football

Why this matters

Evening fixtures, travel, adrenaline, pain and irregular routines can disrupt sleep. Poor sleep can affect reaction time, mood, recovery and performance.

Signs to look out for

  • Difficulty falling or staying asleep
  • Daytime fatigue
  • Poor concentration or slower reactions
  • Irritability or low mood
  • Increasing reliance on caffeine, alcohol or medication

Things you can do today

  • Keep regular sleep and wake times where possible
  • Create a wind-down routine after evening fixtures
  • Reduce caffeine later in the day
  • Avoid alcohol as a sleep aid
  • Seek medical advice for persistent insomnia or heavy snoring

When should I seek help?

  • Symptoms have lasted for around two weeks or more
  • They are affecting football, work, education, relationships or daily life
  • They are getting worse or your usual coping strategies are not helping
  • You are worried about yourself or somebody else

Support inside football

  • Club medical or performance staff
  • Manager or coach
  • Club Welfare Officer

Support outside football

  • Your GP
  • NHS sleep guidance
  • Mental-health support if worry or low mood is affecting sleep

Gambling and betting harms

Football's connection with betting can make gambling feel normal. Harm can affect finances, relationships, mental health, employment and integrity responsibilities.

You may find this page helpful if…

  • You are gambling more than you planned
  • You are chasing losses or hiding gambling
  • Money worries are increasing
  • Gambling is affecting football, work or relationships

In football

Why this matters

People working in football may be subject to strict betting rules. Gambling can also create disciplinary, safeguarding and financial concerns.

Signs to look out for

  • Chasing losses
  • Hiding gambling
  • Borrowing money to bet
  • Betting to escape stress or low mood
  • Missing training, work or family commitments

Things you can do today

  • Stop and put practical blocks in place
  • Tell someone you trust
  • Do not borrow money to recover losses
  • Use bank, device and self-exclusion controls
  • Seek specialist help early

When should I seek help?

  • Symptoms have lasted for around two weeks or more
  • They are affecting football, work, education, relationships or daily life
  • They are getting worse or your usual coping strategies are not helping
  • You are worried about yourself or somebody else

Alcohol and drugs

Alcohol and drug misuse can affect sleep, mood, judgement, relationships, fitness, conduct and injury recovery.

You may find this page helpful if…

  • You are drinking or using drugs more often
  • You use alcohol or drugs to cope, relax or sleep
  • Your mood, relationships or performance are affected
  • You are worried about medication or supplement use

In football

Why this matters

Substance use may affect attendance, performance, safety and anti-doping responsibilities. It can also become a way of coping with stress, injury or low mood.

Signs to look out for

  • Using more often or in larger amounts
  • Needing alcohol or drugs to relax, cope or sleep
  • Mood changes, irritability or aggression
  • Declining attendance, motivation or performance
  • Hiding use or experiencing financial, legal or relationship problems

Things you can do today

  • Avoid recreational drugs
  • Stay within UK low-risk alcohol guidance
  • Do not mix alcohol with medication without professional advice
  • Seek medical advice before suddenly stopping if dependent
  • Check medication and supplements against anti-doping requirements

When should I seek help?

  • Symptoms have lasted for around two weeks or more
  • They are affecting football, work, education, relationships or daily life
  • They are getting worse or your usual coping strategies are not helping
  • You are worried about yourself or somebody else

Support inside football

  • Club Welfare Officer
  • Club medical team
  • Trusted manager or senior club contact

Support outside football

  • Your GP or local NHS alcohol and drug service
  • FRANK — 0300 123 6600
  • Alcoholics Anonymous
  • WithYou

Helping someone else

You do not need to be an expert. A calm conversation, listening carefully and helping someone reach the right support can make a real difference.

Signs somebody may be struggling

  • Changes in mood, behaviour, attendance or appearance
  • Withdrawal from teammates, colleagues, friends or family
  • Loss of confidence, motivation or enjoyment
  • Increased alcohol, drug or gambling behaviour
  • Comments about hopelessness, self-harm or suicide

How to start the conversation

  • Choose a private and suitable time
  • Say what you have noticed without judging them
  • Ask: “How are you really doing?”
  • Listen more than you speak
  • Ask what support would feel helpful
  • Check in again afterwards

What not to do

  • Do not dismiss their feelings or tell them simply to be stronger
  • Do not promise secrecy where somebody may be at risk
  • Do not try to diagnose them
  • Do not leave somebody alone when there is immediate danger

Where to get help

  • Club Welfare Officer
  • Trusted manager, coach or senior club contact
  • Club medical or player-care staff
  • GP, NHS 111 or a specialist support service
  • 999 or A&E in an emergency

Remember

Asking directly about suicide does not put the idea into somebody's head. If you are concerned, ask clearly, listen calmly and seek urgent help when needed.
