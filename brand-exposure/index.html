How a DAZN board is actually found

  1. Match. Every sampled frame is searched for DAZN's keypoints. When 7 or more features agree on the same position and the same perspective, that's a confirmed sighting — the board is outlined on the frame.
  2. Bridge. A fast camera pan blurs boards for a second or two. Rather than losing the board, the scanner carries the last confirmed patch across the gap and checks it still matches on the far side.
  3. Discard the fakes. Anything pinned to the screen rather than the pitch — score bug, channel watermark — sits in the same spot all match. That's fingerprinted as screen furniture and thrown out.
  4. Log. Each sighting records the board's size, sharpness, and how much of its face is actually visible versus blocked by players or staff.

Destination: the Matches tab

Every measured match lands in this tool, in the same shape, comparable with every other — per sponsor, per board, per ground.

seconds on screen exposure index (clarity-weighted) % of time part-blocked the video, boards outlined

HowSeconds = confirmed sightings × the sampling interval. The exposure index weights each second by how big and how sharp the board was on screen; the blocked figure counts sightings where less than 60% of the board's face was clearly visible.

How much to trust the numbers The scanner is marked against a human hand-count of a real match. When it claims a board was on screen, it is right 99% of the time (measured, not estimated). It currently finds 64% of the true seconds — the miss is concentrated in boards that are tiny in very wide shots — so the totals it reports are a floor: read every figure as “at least”. Undercounting is consistent, which keeps match-to-match and ground-to-ground comparisons fair.

Marked like homework, improved like an engine

The scanner is graded against a human hand-count of a real match — 73 timed board appearances, written down by eye. Every version below exists because that marking caught something. Nothing ships until the same marking proves it better.
