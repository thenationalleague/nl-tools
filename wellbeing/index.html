If you do not feel safe

Mental health crisis and suicidal thoughts

Suicidal thoughts can feel frightening and overwhelming. They can pass, and support is available. Tell somebody clearly that you do not feel safe.

Call 999 now when

  • You or another person is about to act on suicidal thoughts
  • An overdose has been taken or serious injury has occurred
  • The person cannot be kept safe
  • There is an immediate danger to another person
999 Emergency services

Take these steps now

  • Stay with another person
  • Move away from anything that could be used to cause harm
  • Avoid alcohol and non-prescribed drugs
  • Call 999, NHS 111, Samaritans or Papyrus as appropriate
  • Do not agree to keep suicidal thoughts secret

Who to call

Samaritans

Free, day or night, from any phone. You do not have to be suicidal to call.

Call 116 123

Struggling today

Urgent mental health support

I am really struggling and need to talk today.

Use this page when you are struggling to cope today but there is no immediate threat to life. Tell someone you trust and do not stay isolated. If you do not feel safe, go to the crisis page instead.

What to do today

  • Call NHS 111, or use NHS 111 online, for urgent mental health help
  • Call Samaritans free on 116 123, day or night
  • Contact your GP, club doctor, medical lead or Club Welfare Officer
  • Ask somebody to stay with you, or to help you make the call

Where to start

Understanding your mental wellbeing

Something has changed and I am not sure why.

Mental wellbeing affects how you think, feel, cope and connect with other people. Football can support wellbeing, but injury, selection, contracts, travel, work, study, relationships and money can also create pressure. You do not need a diagnosis before speaking to someone.

How might it affect a player?

  • Football does not feel enjoyable
  • You are worrying more or becoming irritable
  • You are withdrawing from teammates, family or friends
  • Your sleep, appetite or concentration has changed
  • You are using alcohol, drugs or gambling to switch off

What can I do today?

  • Notice what has changed and when it began
  • Tell one trusted person
  • Keep regular meals, sleep and contact with others
  • Reduce avoidable pressure, including social media
  • Choose the topic page that best fits how you feel

When should I get help?

  • Changes last for around two weeks or are getting worse
  • Football, work, education or relationships are affected
  • People close to you are worried
  • You feel hopeless, unsafe or unable to cope

I'm worried about a teammate

Ask how they are, describe what you have noticed and listen. You do not need to diagnose them. Encourage them to speak to the welfare or medical team. There is more on helping a teammate.

Topic

Anxiety

I cannot stop worrying.

Anxiety is a natural response to pressure or uncertainty. It becomes a problem when worry feels difficult to control or starts affecting daily life, sleep, relationships or football.

How might it affect a player?

  • Overthinking mistakes or selection decisions
  • Feeling sick, tense or panicky before training or matches
  • Racing thoughts, tight chest or fast breathing
  • Avoiding teammates, coaches or situations
  • Difficulty sleeping or switching off

What can I do today?

  • Slow your breathing and focus on what is happening now
  • Write down the specific worry rather than carrying it all day
  • Reduce caffeine and take a break from hostile social media
  • Tell someone you trust
  • Contact a GP or NHS talking therapies if it continues

When should I get help?

  • Anxiety continues or gets worse
  • You are avoiding football or daily life
  • You are having panic attacks
  • You are using substances or gambling to cope
  • You feel hopeless or unsafe

I'm worried about a teammate

Do not tell them simply to calm down. Ask what would help, listen and offer to go with them to speak to a welfare or health professional.

Topic

Burnout

I am exhausted and football feels like a chore.

Burnout can develop after prolonged physical and emotional pressure without enough recovery. It may affect motivation, confidence, enjoyment and relationships.

How might it affect a player?

  • Emotional and physical exhaustion
  • Loss of enjoyment or motivation
  • Feeling detached or cynical
  • Irritability and reduced concentration
  • Rest no longer feels restorative

What can I do today?

  • Tell the medical or welfare team
  • Review workload, travel, work, study and recovery
  • Take genuine time away from football demands
  • Maintain contact with supportive people
  • Seek professional help where low mood or anxiety is present

When should I get help?

  • Symptoms continue despite rest
  • You dread football or daily responsibilities
  • You are repeatedly injured or unwell
  • You feel hopeless, numb or unsafe

I'm worried about a teammate

Avoid describing burnout as laziness. Encourage rest, assessment and an honest discussion about demands and recovery.

Topic

Depression and low mood

I do not feel like myself anymore.

Everyone feels low sometimes. Depression is more persistent and can affect motivation, enjoyment, energy, sleep, appetite and hope. It is not a weakness or a lack of effort.

How might it affect a player?

  • Losing interest in football or other activities
  • Feeling low, empty, numb or hopeless
  • Withdrawing from other people
  • Changes in sleep, appetite or concentration
  • Feeling worthless or like a burden

What can I do today?

  • Tell somebody how long you have felt this way
  • Keep basic routines for sleep, meals and contact
  • Book a GP appointment
  • Avoid isolating yourself after training or matches
  • Seek urgent help if you feel unsafe

When should I get help?

  • Symptoms persist for two weeks or more
  • Daily life or football is affected
  • You feel unable to cope
  • You have thoughts of self-harm or suicide

I'm worried about a teammate

Notice changes rather than judging performance. Ask directly how they are feeling and whether they feel safe. Take references to suicide seriously.

Topic

Panic attacks

My body feels out of control.

A panic attack is a sudden surge of intense fear with strong physical sensations. It can feel dangerous, but the symptoms usually pass. Medical assessment is important when symptoms are new or unclear.

How might it affect a player?

  • Racing heart, shaking or sweating
  • Fast breathing, dizziness or tingling
  • Chest discomfort or nausea
  • Feeling detached or afraid of losing control
  • Fear of another attack

What can I do today?

  • Move to a quieter safe place
  • Breathe slowly, with a longer breath out
  • Focus on five things you can see and four you can feel
  • Remind yourself that the feeling will pass
  • Arrange GP support if attacks repeat

When should I get help?

  • It is your first episode, or symptoms could be physical illness
  • Attacks are recurring
  • You avoid activities because of fear
  • You feel unsafe or use substances to cope

I'm worried about a teammate

Stay calm, reduce the audience around them, encourage slow breathing and ask what they need. Seek emergency help if symptoms suggest a medical emergency.

Topic

Self-harm

I have been hurting myself to cope.

Self-harm is when somebody deliberately injures or harms themselves. It is often a way of coping with overwhelming feelings. It should always be taken seriously and met without judgement.

How might it affect a player?

  • Unexplained cuts, burns or injuries
  • Covering the body unexpectedly
  • Withdrawing or becoming secretive
  • Carrying items that may be used for harm
  • Feeling shame or saying they deserve pain

What can I do today?

  • Tell somebody you trust
  • Move away from items used to harm yourself
  • Use urgent support if you may harm yourself again
  • Contact your GP or mental health service
  • Treat injuries and seek medical help when needed

When should I get help?

  • There is serious injury, heavy bleeding or overdose
  • You cannot keep yourself safe
  • Self-harm is becoming more frequent or severe
  • You also have suicidal thoughts

I'm worried about a teammate

Remain calm. Do not demand to see injuries or threaten punishment. Ask whether they are safe, arrange medical care and follow safeguarding procedures for children or adults at risk.

If they are under 18, tell your club's Designated Safeguarding Officer and contact the National League safeguarding leads at the foot of this page. Do not agree to keep it to yourself, however much you want to.

Topic

Sleep problems

I cannot switch off or recover.

Sleep supports mood, concentration, decision-making and physical recovery. Travel, evening matches, pain, anxiety, caffeine and irregular routines can all affect it.

How might it affect a player?

  • Difficulty falling or staying asleep
  • Waking too early
  • Feeling exhausted despite time in bed
  • Poor concentration and irritability
  • Using alcohol or medication to sleep

What can I do today?

  • Keep a consistent waking time
  • Reduce caffeine later in the day
  • Create a wind-down period after evening football
  • Keep the phone away from the bed
  • Speak to a GP about persistent sleep problems or breathing concerns

When should I get help?

  • Problems persist for several weeks
  • Sleepiness makes driving or work unsafe
  • You are relying on alcohol or tablets
  • You snore heavily, choke or stop breathing
  • Poor sleep is linked to severe low mood

I'm worried about a teammate

Encourage support rather than teasing them about tiredness. Long-term sleep loss can be a sign of pain, anxiety, depression or another health issue.

Topic

Stress

Everything feels too much.

Stress is the body and mind responding to demands. Short periods can be manageable, but constant pressure can affect health, decisions, performance and relationships.

How might it affect a player?

  • Feeling tense, impatient or overwhelmed
  • Headaches, stomach problems or poor sleep
  • Difficulty concentrating or making decisions
  • Arguments with teammates or family
  • Feeling that every task is urgent

What can I do today?

  • List what must be done today and what can wait
  • Speak to a coach or manager about realistic expectations
  • Protect rest and recovery time
  • Take short breaks from your phone
  • Ask for help with work, study, money or family pressure

When should I get help?

  • Stress is constant or worsening
  • Physical symptoms are persistent
  • You are making risky decisions
  • You cannot function normally
  • Stress is leading to alcohol, drugs or gambling

I'm worried about a teammate

Offer practical help, not just advice. Help them separate immediate problems from things that can wait and encourage professional support when needed.

Supporting someone else

Helping a teammate

You do not need to be an expert.

Noticing a change, asking a direct question and helping someone reach support can make a significant difference.

Start the conversation

  • “You do not seem yourself lately. How are you?”
  • “I noticed you have become quieter. Is something worrying you?”
  • “Are you thinking about harming yourself or suicide?”
  • “What would help you get through today?”

Do

  • Listen without judgement
  • Take references to suicide seriously
  • Help them contact support
  • Stay with them if immediate risk is suspected
  • Share safeguarding concerns with the appropriate professional

Do not

  • Promise secrecy
  • Investigate an abuse allegation yourself
  • Dismiss it as attention-seeking
  • Leave a person alone when immediate risk is suspected

If they are under 18

You must pass it on. A concern about anyone under 18, or an adult at risk, goes to your club's Designated Safeguarding Officer and to the National League safeguarding leads at the foot of this page. This is a duty, not a judgement call, and it is not a betrayal of their trust. Say plainly at the start that there are some things you cannot keep to yourself.

They can also contact Childline themselves, free and confidentially, on 0800 1111.
